    Local vs formal

    Interpreters switch register, not nationality. The left column is what people say. The right column is what belongs in a record.

    Situation Local Formal
    Agreeing Yeah / yep / no worries / grand / sure Yes. / I agree. / That is acceptable.
    Time off vacation (US) · holiday (UK/AU/IE) · annual leave I will be on annual leave / vacation from…
    Bathroom restroom / bathroom (US) · loo / toilet (UK) · washroom (CA) restroom / toilet facilities
    Soon in a bit · just now (ZA, not immediately) · now-now (ZA, sooner) shortly / presently / in a few minutes
    Reply later I’ll get back to you · I’ll revert (IN) I will respond in writing. / I will reply to your message.
    Money bucks · quid · bucks (AU) · lakh / crore (IN) dollars / pounds / rupees, with the figure written out

    Grammar that changes by country

    These are standard in one variety. Do not mark them as errors. On a record, keep the speaker’s form unless the listener will read the wrong time or the wrong person.

    Feature Where What it means If the listener will misread it
    gotten US Normal past participle of get. UK record: have got / have become.
    the team are UK, IE, AU, NZ, ZA, IN Collective noun with plural verb is normal. US record can keep it. It is not an agreement error.
    in hospital / at university UK, IE, AU, NZ, IN No article when the institution is the role, not the building. US: in the hospital / in college. Same meaning.
    I’m after booking it IE I have just booked it. Completed recent past. Do not render as a future plan.
    just now ZA Often later, not immediately. now-now is sooner. Give a time. Do not write immediately.
    I am knowing / I will revert IN Stative progressive and revert = reply. Official Indian English. For a US/UK listener: I understand / I will reply.
    She done gone / soon come CB Creole aspect: completed action / arrival later. She has already left / she will arrive shortly.
    3/4/26 US vs everyone else US month-day. UK-aligned day-month. Write 4 March 2026 or 3 April 2026. Never leave the slash date.
    first floor US vs UK-aligned US: street level. UK/IE/AU/NZ/IN: one flight up. Say ground floor or second storey.

    Trap words

    Same word, different meaning, or socially risky. Formal English usually picks the unambiguous word.

    • pants — US: trousers. UK/IE: often underwear. Formal: trousers.
    • rubber — US classroom: eraser. UK: condom. Formal: eraser.
    • root — AU slang can be vulgar. Formal: support / cheer for or name the plant.
    • table a motion — US: postpone. UK: bring it up. Formal: say postpone or place on the agenda.
    • public school — US: state-funded. UK: fee-paying. Formal: state school or independent school.
    • first floor — US: ground level. UK/IE/AU/IN: one flight up. Formal: say ground floor or second storey.
    • just now (ZA) — not immediately; later. now-now — sooner. Formal: give a time.
    • revert (IN) — reply. US/UK: go back to a previous state. Formal outside India: I will reply.
    • thongs (AU/NZ) — flip-flops. US: underwear. Formal: flip-flops / sandals.
    • grand (IE) — fine, all right. US: a thousand dollars. Formal: that is acceptable vs $1,000.

    Regional profiles

    Countries first. Inside each country, regions still differ. Use the picker for a pair; use this for the map.

    US

    United States

    Local: rhotic speech is common; apartment, elevator, truck, vacation, pants, fries. Formal: -or/-ize spelling; insurance and court jargon is Latinate. Inside the country: the South, New York, the Midwest, and the West are not one accent. African American English is a systematic variety, not “incorrect English.” Do not flatten it when interpreting.

    UK

    United Kingdom

    Local: non-rhotic accents are common in England; flat, lift, lorry, holiday, trousers, chips. Formal: -our/-re spelling; NHS and court terms differ from the US. Inside the country: Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland, and Northern England are not “London English.” Scots and Scottish English are distinct. Keep the speaker’s words.

    IE

    Ireland

    Local: grand = fine; ye as plural you; press = cupboard; craic = a good time. Spelling is UK-aligned. Formal: close to UK official English; Irish legal and health systems have their own names. Do not treat Irish English as a cute UK accent.

    AU

    Australia

    Local: arvo, ute, thongs (flip-flops), no worries. Chips can be fries or crisps. Formal: UK-aligned spelling; government and court English is close to UK. Do not “correct” slang into US English when interpreting.

    NZ

    New Zealand

    Local: close to Australian slang, with its own words: jandals (flip-flops), bach (holiday house), dairy (corner shop). Vowel quality differs from AU. Formal: UK-aligned. Māori names and terms belong in the record as said.

    CA

    Canada

    Local: speech often sounds US; washroom, toque, eh. Formal: writing often uses colour, centre; courts and health are provincial. Inside the country: Quebec English sits next to French. Atlantic and Prairie speech are not Toronto.

    ZA

    South Africa

    Local: robot = traffic light; bakkie = pickup; braai = barbecue; just now ≠ immediately. Formal: UK-aligned spelling; official English sits beside Afrikaans, isiZulu, isiXhosa, and other languages. Keep those names. South African English is not “British with an accent.”

    IN

    India

    Local: prepone, revert, good name, only, itself, lakh, crore. These are Indian English, not mistakes. Formal: a recognized official variety in courts and administration, often UK-aligned in spelling. Do not rewrite a clear Indian English sentence into US or UK idiom unless the listener will misunderstand.

    CB

    Caribbean English

    Local: Jamaica, Trinidad, Barbados, the Bahamas, and others each have their own English and Creole continuum. Everyday speech may use Creole grammar; that is a system, not broken English. Formal: school and court English is often UK-aligned. Interpreting: render the meaning. Do not “fix” aspect markers or pronoun forms if the sense is clear.

    Don’t “correct” this

    In interpreting, convey meaning. Do not standardize the speaker’s country or their register unless the listener will not understand.

    • UK colour / US color is not an error.
    • Indian English prepone and revert are variety features.
    • Caribbean Creole grammar is not “broken English.”
    • South African just now is not a failed “right now.”
    • Irish grand is not a dollar amount.
    • If the speaker is consistent in their variety, keep their lexical choices.
