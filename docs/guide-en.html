English for Spanish & French speakers
Contrastive rules for interpreters and learners whose L1 is Spanish or French. Each section flags the typical transfer error and the fix.
Articles · A1

a / an / the / zero article

A1

English marks definiteness more often than Spanish or French generics.

The Core Rule

a/an = first mention, one of many. the = known, unique, or previously mentioned. Zero article with plural/uncountable generics (Dogs bark, Water is essential) — not the dogs bark in general statements.

🇪🇸
From Spanish
Me gusta la música. → I like music. (not *the music* as a general taste)
El paciente tiene fiebre. → The patient has a fever. (countable illness often needs a)
🇫🇷
From French
J’aime le café. → I like coffee. (not *the coffee* for the drink in general)
C’est un problème. → It’s a problem. (keep a/an; don’t drop it)
Present · A1

Present simple vs continuous

A1

Habits and facts vs actions happening now (or temporary).

The Core Rule

I work = habit / job / truth. I am working = right now or temporary. Romance speakers often overuse -ing for habits.

Questions & negation · A1

Do-support

A1

English needs do/does/did for most questions and negatives in the simple tenses.

The Core Rule

Questions: Do you speak English? / Does she work here? / Did they arrive? Negatives: I don’t know, She doesn’t live here. Do not calque Spanish/French inversion alone (*Speak you English?*).

Past · A2

Past simple vs present perfect

A2

Finished time vs experience / unfinished time.

The Core Rule

Past simple with finished time: yesterday, last week, in 2019, ago → I went yesterday. Present perfect for experience or result with present relevance: I have been to London, She has already signed. Spanish pretérito/perfecto and French passé composé map imperfectly — time markers decide.

⏱️
Finished time → past simple
I saw the doctor yesterday.
🔗
Present link → present perfect
I have already seen the doctor.
Nouns · A2

Countable vs uncountable

A2

much/many, a little/a few, information, advice, news.

The Core Rule

information, advice, news, furniture, homework are uncountable in English — some advice, not *an advice*; the news is, not *are*. False friends with ES información / FR information (countable in those languages).

Prepositions · A2

Preposition traps from Spanish & French

A2
High-frequency fixes

depend on (not *of*). married to (not *with*). listen to (not bare *listen*). discuss (no *about*). arrive at/in (not *to* for cities: arrive in Madrid). FR: interested in (not *by* from intéressé par).

Conditionals · B1

Real and unreal conditionals

B1
The Core Rule

Real: If + present, will… If she calls, I will answer. Unreal present: If + past, would… If I had time, I would help. Never put would in the if-clause (*If I would have…*). Do not copy Spanish/French conditional into the si/if clause.

Modals · B1

Modals for interpreters

B1
Precision matters

must / have to = obligation. should = advice. may/might = possibility. can = ability / permission. In clinical/legal English, prefer the precise modal over soft paraphrase.

Verb patterns · B1

Gerund vs infinitive

B1
The Core Rule

enjoy / finish / avoid / suggest + -ing. decide / hope / plan / refuse + to. Some change meaning: stop smoking vs stop to smoke. Memorize high-frequency verbs; don’t guess from Spanish/French infinitive patterns.

Voice · B2

Passive for formality

B2
The Core Rule

be + past participle: The patient was informed. Useful in clinical/legal English when the agent is unknown or less important. Keep tense on be.

Vocabulary · B2

Phrasal verbs vs Latinate verbs

B2
Register tip

Everyday: find out, put off, bring up. Professional interpreting often prefers Latinate: discover, postpone, mention. Match the speaker’s register; don’t “upgrade” casual speech into legalese unless the setting requires it.

Register · C1

Professional register

C1
Interpreter English

Prefer clear Latinate verbs in clinical/legal settings, hedge carefully (appears to, may indicate), and keep speaker intent. Avoid calques: depend on, discuss X, interested in. Consistency of formality across the turn matters more than “sounding fancy.”

Discourse · C2

Hedging and cohesion

C2
Near-native control

Link ideas with however, therefore, in contrast, as a result. Soften claims when the speaker softens them. Preserve register shifts between emotional speech and formal testimony — do not flatten them into one tone.
