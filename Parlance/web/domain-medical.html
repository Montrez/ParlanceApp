Ethics

The five core canons

These mirror the standards medical interpreters are certified against (CCHI, NBCMI, NCIHC Code of Ethics).

🎯
Accuracy & completeness
Interpret everything said, including tone and hesitation, without adding, omitting, or softening — even if the content is upsetting (a diagnosis, a prognosis).
⚖️
Impartiality
Don't advocate for the treatment plan, don't take the patient's or provider's side in a disagreement, and disclose personal relationships before interpreting.
🔒
Confidentiality (HIPAA)
All patient information is protected health information — do not discuss it outside the encounter, including with family members not present or authorized.
🧭
Role boundaries
You are not a cultural broker, advisor, or clinician. Don't explain medical concepts in your own words, recommend treatment, or accept a family member's request to "just summarize."
🗣️
Professionalism & scope
Interpret in the first person, disclose when a term is unclear or requires repetition, and never fill in gaps with your own medical knowledge.
Register

Always formal, and the three modes

Core rule

Default to formal address (usted / vous / formal English). Prefer precise clinical terms over slang, but be ready to render a patient's folk/colloquial description accurately rather than "upgrading" it to clinical language they didn't use.

ModeWhen it's used
ConsecutiveThe default for clinical encounters — provider or patient pauses after a segment, you render it fully.
SimultaneousUsed in fast-moving settings (ER trauma, surgery consent read aloud to a group) so care isn't delayed.
Sight translationReading discharge instructions, a consent form, or intake paperwork aloud in the other language — read the whole document first.
Structure

The clinical encounter, step by step

1️⃣
Chief complaint
The patient's own words for why they came in today — render it as stated, not paraphrased into clinical shorthand.
2️⃣
History of present illness (HPI)
Detailed symptom timeline — this is where the OPQRST technique below is used.
3️⃣
Review of systems (ROS)
A checklist run through body systems (cardiac, respiratory, GI, etc.) — expect rapid-fire yes/no questions.
4️⃣
Physical exam
Provider narrates instructions ("take a deep breath", "does this hurt?") — short, simultaneous-style renditions work best here.
5️⃣
Diagnosis & plan
This is where informed consent and treatment options are discussed — accuracy matters most here since decisions follow directly from it.
6️⃣
Discharge instructions
Medication schedule, follow-up, warning signs to return — often sight-translated from a printed handout.

Traps

False cognates that can cause real harm

High stakes
Medical false cognates are the single most-documented cause of interpreting errors that change patient care — memorize these.
Word
What it actually means
intoxicado (ES)
Means poisoned/had an adverse reaction to something ingested — NOT drunk/intoxicated on alcohol. A famous, real malpractice case turned on this exact confusion.
constipado (ES)
Means "has a cold" (nasal congestion) in most of Latin America/Spain — NOT constipated (bowel-related). Confirm which system before charting.
embarazada (ES)
Means pregnant — not "embarrassed". Well-known trap for English speakers going the other direction; won't trip up ES→EN, but confirm the reverse doesn't happen mid-session.
dilate (EN) vs. dilatar (ES)
Cognate is fine for pupils/cervix, but in casual Spanish "dilatar" can also mean "to delay/take a while" — confirm the anatomical sense is intended in ambiguous phrasing.
grippe (FR)
"Grippe" in French means influenza/flu specifically, not a general cold ("rhume") and not "grip" (hand strength) — don't conflate the three.

Specialties

Notes by specialty area

  • OB/GYN — Extra care with modesty/register norms; some patients prefer a same-gender interpreter for this domain if one is available. Miscarriage/loss terminology should be rendered gently but exactly, never euphemized beyond what the speaker said.
  • Pediatrics — Two audiences at once (parent + child); interpret what's said to each without merging them into one rendition, and expect growth/milestone vocabulary (percentile, developmental delay).
  • Mental health — Register and tone carry as much meaning as words; avoid clinicizing a patient's own description of distress, and expect to interpret screening-tool questions verbatim (e.g. depression/anxiety scales).
  • Emergency / trauma — Fast, high-stakes, often simultaneous; short renditions, no elaboration, and be ready for triage-style rapid questions (allergies, medications, last meal).
Fixed phrases

Renditions to memorize verbatim

Informed consent opening
'I am going to explain this procedure, its risks, benefits, and alternatives. Please stop me if you have any questions.' → ES: 'Voy a explicarle este procedimiento, sus riesgos, beneficios y alternativas. Por favor, deténgame si tiene alguna pregunta.' → FR: « Je vais vous expliquer cette procédure, ses risques, ses bénéfices et ses alternatives. N'hésitez pas à m'arrêter si vous avez des questions. »
Discharge warning-sign phrasing
'Return to the emergency room immediately if you experience ___.' Render the listed symptoms exactly and completely — this is the clause most likely to affect patient safety if shortened.
Practice

Journal prompts

  • Describe a patient's chief complaint and how long symptoms have lasted.
  • Explain medication instructions (dose, frequency, with/without food).
  • Interpret a provider asking about allergies and current medications.
  • Walk through an OPQRST symptom history for a headache using each of the six prompts above.
  • Sight-translate a short discharge instruction sheet into your practice language.
  • Write a journal entry using at least three terms from the medications table above, including a dosage instruction.
