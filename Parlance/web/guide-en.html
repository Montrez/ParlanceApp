Essential verbs · A1

Be, have, there is / there are

A1

The first verbs you need in every sentence.

The Core Rule

Be (am / is / are) names identity, age, nationality, and location with there. Have (have / has) shows possession and some states (I have a fever). Use there is (singular) and there are (plural) to introduce something new.

Articles · A1

a / an / the / zero article

A1

English marks definiteness more often than many other languages do with generics.

The Core Rule

a/an = first mention, one of many (an before a vowel sound: an hour, an MRI). the = known, unique, or already mentioned. Zero article with plural or uncountable generics: Dogs bark. Water is essential. Not the dogs bark for a general truth.

Present · A1

A1

Habits and facts vs actions happening now, or temporary.

I work = habit, job, or general truth. Add -s for he/she/it: She works nights. I am working = right now or a temporary situation. Do not use -ing for habits. Stative verbs rarely take -ing: know, want, like, need, believe, belong.

Questions and negation · A1

Do-support

A1

English needs do / does / did for most questions and negatives in the simple tenses.

The Core Rule

Questions: Do you speak English? Does she work here? Did they arrive? Negatives: I don't know. She doesn't live here. We didn't sign. Do not invert the main verb alone (*Speak you English?). Be, modals, and auxiliaries invert themselves: Is she ready? Can you wait? Have they left?

Pronouns · A1

Pronouns and possessives

A1

Subject, object, possessive adjective, possessive pronoun, reflexive.

The Core Rule

Subject does the action (She called). Object receives it (I called her). Possessive adjectives sit before a noun (her file). Possessive pronouns stand alone (The file is hers). Reflexives end in -self / -selves when the subject and object are the same (He introduced himself).

Nouns · A1

Plurals and demonstratives

A1

Regular -s, a short irregular list, and this / that / these / those.

The Core Rule

Most nouns add -s (forms, patients). Add -es after s/sh/ch/x/z (boxes, watches). Change y → ies after a consonant (cities). Irregulars you must memorize: man/men, woman/women, child/children, person/people, foot/feet, tooth/teeth. This/that are singular. These/those are plural. Adjectives do not take a plural: two new files, not *news files.

NearFar
this formthat form
these formsthose forms
Adjective order
English adjectives go before the noun: a medical interpreter, not *an interpreter medical.

Nouns · A2

Countable vs uncountable

A2

much / many, a little / a few, information, advice, news.

The Core Rule

Countable nouns have a plural and take a/an, many, a few, these. Uncountable nouns have no plural and take some, much, a little, this. High-frequency uncountables: information, advice, news, furniture, homework, work, research, evidence, equipment, luggage, traffic, weather. Say some advice, not *an advice. The news is, not *are.

Prepositions · A2

Preposition traps

A2

Time, place, and the verb + preposition pairs that do not calque.

The Core Rule

in = months, years, cities, countries, rooms. on = days, dates, surfaces. at = clock times, points, events. High-frequency verb pairs: depend on, married to, listen to, wait for, look at, arrive in (city) / at (building), interested in, discuss X (no *about), enter (no *in).

Future · A2

Will, going to, present continuous

A2

A decision now, a plan already made, or a fixed arrangement.

The Core Rule

will = a decision at the moment of speaking, a promise, or a prediction: I'll call her now. be going to = a plan or evidence you can see: I'm going to file this afternoon. Look at those clouds. It's going to rain. Present continuous = a fixed arrangement, often with a time: I'm meeting the attorney at 3. Present simple for timetables: The hearing starts at 9.

Comparison · A2

Comparatives and superlatives

A2

taller than, more careful than, the most careful.

The Core Rule

One syllable: tall → taller → the tallest. Two syllables ending in -y: easy → easier → the easiest. Longer adjectives: more / the most careful. Irregulars: good/better/best, bad/worse/worst, far/farther|further/farthest|furthest. Use than after a comparative, not *that. Use as … as for equality: as clear as the original.

Conditionals · B1

Zero, first, second, third

B1

Real, unreal present, and unreal past. Never put would in the if-clause.

The Core Rule

Zero (always true): If + present, present. If you heat ice, it melts. First (real future): If + present, will. If she calls, I will answer. Second (unreal now): If + past, would. If I had time, I would help. Third (unreal past): If + past perfect, would have. If I had known, I would have called. Never *If I would have…

TypeIf-clauseResult
0If you press thisthe light turns on
1If she arrives latewe will start without her
2If I were youI would ask for counsel
3If they had objectedthe judge would have paused
Were in the second conditional
Formal English uses if I were / if he were (not was) for unreal present.
Modals · B1

Modals for interpreters

B1

Obligation, advice, possibility, ability. Precision matters in clinical and legal English.

The Core Rule

Modals do not take -s, to, or do: She can wait. not *She cans / *She can to wait. The meaning sits on the modal. In clinical and legal English, keep the speaker's force. Do not soften must into should unless the speaker did.

ModalMeaningExample

may / mightpossibility, formal permissionIt may indicate infection.
must / have toobligation, strong deductionYou must remain silent.
should / ought toadviceYou should follow up in a week.
wouldhypothetical, polite requestWould you state your name?
mustn'tprohibitionYou mustn't discuss the case.
don't have tono obligation (not prohibition)You don't have to answer that.
mustn't vs don't have to
mustn't = not allowed. don't have to = not required. These are opposites.

Clauses · B1

Relative clauses

B1

who, which, that, whose, where. Defining vs extra information.

The Core Rule

who = people. which = things. that = people or things in defining clauses. whose = possession. where = place. A defining clause identifies which one and has no commas: The witness who spoke first was brief. A non-defining clause adds extra information and needs commas: The witness, who spoke first, was brief. You cannot use that after a comma.

Speech · B1

Reported speech

B1

She said she needed time. Tense usually steps back.

The Core Rule

say does not take a person object: She said (that) she was ready. tell needs a person: She told me she was ready. When the reporting verb is past, tenses usually backshift: present → past, will → would, can → could, past simple / present perfect → past perfect. Time words often shift too: today → that day, tomorrow → the next day, here → there. Questions become statements: He asked if I was ready. not *He asked was I ready.

"I will sign."He said he would sign.
"I have finished."They said they had finished.
"Can you wait?"She asked if I could wait.
"Where do you live?"He asked where I lived.
Past habits · B1

Used to / would

B1

Past habits and states that are no longer true.

The Core Rule

used to covers past habits and past states: I used to live in Lyon. I used to take the bus. would covers past habits only, not states: Every summer we would visit my aunt. not *I would live in Lyon. Negative and question: didn't use to / Did you use to…? (no -d).

Voice · B2

Passive voice

B2

be + past participle. The tense sits on be.

The Core Rule

Use the passive when the agent is unknown, obvious, or less important than the action: The patient was informed. The motion was denied. Add by only if the agent matters: The order was signed by the judge. Keep the tense on be. In clinical and legal English the passive is common. Do not use it to hide a speaker who is taking responsibility.

Past · B2

Past perfect

B2

had + past participle. The earlier of two past events.

The Core Rule

Use past perfect when you are already in the past and you need to name an even earlier action: When I arrived, the hearing had already started. Do not use it for a single finished past event with a time marker. That is past simple: The hearing started at 9.

Unreal · B2

Wish / if only

B2

Regret now, regret then, and annoyance.

The Core Rule

wish + past = a different present: I wish I knew the answer. wish + past perfect = a different past: I wish I had asked. wish + would = annoyance about someone else's habit: I wish he would stop interrupting. If only is the same grammar with more force.

Register · C1

Professional register

C1

Match the speaker. Do not flatten every turn into one tone.

The Core Rule

Prefer clear Latinate verbs in clinical and legal settings, hedge when the speaker hedges (appears to, may indicate), and keep speaker intent. Avoid calques: depend on, discuss X, interested in. Consistency of formality across the turn matters more than sounding fancy.

Emphasis · C1

Clefts and nominalization

C1

It was X who… What I need is… Turning verbs into nouns for formal tone.

The Core Rule

A cleft highlights one piece of information. It was the nurse who called. (not the doctor). What the patient needs is rest. Nominalization turns a verb into a noun and is common in reports: They decided → their decision. She refused → her refusal. We observed → our observation. Use it for formality. Do not stack so many nouns that the sentence dies.

🎯
It-cleft
It was the second witness who changed the timeline.
Highlights the second witness.
💬
Wh-cleft
What I need is a complete address.
Highlights the needed thing.
📑
Nominalization
The refusal to sign delayed the discharge.
From she refused to sign.
Emphasis · C1

Inversion and emphasis

C1

Never have I… Should you need… Formal and written more than spoken.

The Core Rule

After a negative or restrictive adverb at the start, invert the auxiliary: Never have I seen that. Rarely does the court allow it. Not only did she object, she also asked for a recess. Only then did he sign. Conditional inversion drops if: Should you need help, call. Had I known, I would have waited. Were he here, he would object. Keep this for formal English. Do not invert in casual speech.

Discourse · C2

Hedging and cohesion

C2

Soften claims when the speaker softens them. Link ideas without flattening tone.

The Core Rule

Link ideas with however, therefore, in contrast, as a result, nevertheless, meanwhile. Soften with appears to, may indicate, it is possible that, to the best of my knowledge, as far as I can tell. Preserve register shifts between emotional speech and formal testimony. Do not flatten them into one tone.

Discourse · C2

Discourse and implication

C2

What is said, what is implied, and how English understates.

The Core Rule

English often understates. That's not ideal can mean it is a serious problem. I was a bit concerned can mean alarm. We might want to reconsider can be a refusal. When you interpret, keep the understatement if the speaker used it. Do not upgrade it into a louder English that the speaker did not choose. Also keep discourse markers that organize the turn: look, I mean, the thing is, at the end of the day, to be honest.

🔉
Understatement
That's not ideal. I'm a bit concerned.
Often stronger than it sounds.
🪞
Implication
If I were you, I would get a second opinion.
Advice without a command.
🧵
Markers
Look, the thing is, we don't have the file.
Organizes the turn. Keep them.
⚖️
Interpreter note
Do not make a soft speaker sound certain.
Force belongs to the speaker.
